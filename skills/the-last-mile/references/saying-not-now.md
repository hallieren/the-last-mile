# Saying not now: one sentence rehearsed at four stages

**Load this reference when:** you must choose which idea to build, an executive's pet request or a board-forwarded idea lands on your desk, the sponsor asks for "full automation", someone says "great idea, let's look next quarter", or you have to say no to the person who funds you.

Source: chapters 7, 25 (`docs/chapters/ch07-opportunity.md`, `docs/chapters/ch25-saying-no.md`); templates 7, 25 (`docs/appendices/template-07-five-questions.md`, `docs/appendices/template-25-intake-redlines.md`); template 8 §8.3 (`docs/appendices/template-08-thin-slice.md`); template 19 §19.3 (`docs/appendices/template-19-memo-suite.md`).

The sentence is "the evidence says not yet". Rehearse it at four stages, one instrument each. An idea gets the corridor implication question and then the five-question elimination. An extension gets a scope decision log row. A phase close gets the impact memo's options, each with a revival condition, hold and watch last. A project at the door gets the intake rubric with three red lines. Every instrument does one job. It translates refusal from a relationship event, settled by position and rank, into a judgment event, settled by evidence, and nobody in the room has evidence harder than yours. A no carrying only an attitude gets you routed around, and a team that gets routed around cannot keep its gate.

## Decisions

### Where the right to screen comes from

- The ask belongs to the department that made it and you are not its review committee. Veto on personal judgment and it works once; the second time they go around you, the third time they go to the sponsor. So never say "we are not doing this". Say "this team is not doing this this quarter, on these three pieces of evidence". You hold no veto power. You hold scheduling rights.
- The right has two sources, and neither is granted by you to yourself. One, the intake gate. Every idea enters through the same entrance, the same five-question sheet, the same elimination rule, hung on the company's existing project approval or requirements review. The gate's force comes from which meeting it hangs on, who backs it, and whether it is written into policy. Behind the gate you only fill in the sheet; what gets eliminated is the idea that fails the sheet, not the idea you dislike. Two, the resource pool the sponsor authorized. What he gave you was "what this team works on first this quarter is set by this sheet".
- No gate exists yet → substitute repetition. Same sheet, same rule, same reporting format. By the third time (rule) the rule exists ahead of the individual case, and that is the smallest form of an institution.

### Stage one, an idea

- The proposal has not taken shape yet (a corridor remark, a forwarded article) → ask the implication question in passing, "if this is not fixed, what does it look like three months from now, and who suffers?" Half the ideas fall apart in the proposer's own answer, entering no sheet and no report and offending no one. The cheapest moment to eliminate an idea is before it has become a forwarded email.
- The idea cannot be rewritten as a workflow claim ("this system will change <role and name>'s <next action>") → out in round one, before scoring.
- The idea survives → score five questions, 30 minutes per candidate (rule), evidence only. The five stand in a multiplying relationship, so the logic is weakest link, not sum.

| # | Question | Test question | A 5 | A 3 | A 1 | What the evidence column requires |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pain | Whose action is bleeding? Is the bleed rate (loss per unit of time, hours, complaints, fines, churn) measurable? Follow-up: if it goes unfixed for three months, what happens, who suffers? | A specific person + a specific action + a measured bleed rate; leave it and someone keeps taking blame or paying out | The pain is real, the bleed rate unmeasured; measure first, then come back | Cannot name the person or the action; or the answer to "what if it is not fixed" is "not much" | Users' own words, ticket volume, waiting time, complaint records. "Everyone feels that way" is not allowed |
| 2 | Data | Does the data this action needs exist? Can you get it? Can you trust it? (a pre-check; the full grading is the data fitness ladder) | Data exists, the business owner is named, samples in hand, spot checks hold up | Exists and reachable, trustworthiness unverified; conditional pass with the reconciliation action and its deadline | The key data does not exist, or there is no realistic path to getting it | Data samples, field spot-check results, the name of the data's business owner. "The business unit says it is all there" does not count, nor "we assumed it was all there" |
| 3 | Decision | Which decision point does AI enter? How many times may it be wrong there? Who backstops it, how fast does the error surface? | Enters at the advise layer; backstop named; errors visible on the spot and overridable | A backstop exists, but discovery of the error is delayed or the backstop has not confirmed | Real-time and outward-facing with nobody backstopping; or a near-zero-tolerance decision (money, compliance, safety) executed directly by AI | Where the decision point sits in the real workflow (field archaeology output, not the official flowchart) + the backstop's name |
| 4 | Risk | What does the worst single output cause? Does it reach only inside, or customers, regulators, money? Can the loss be closed out? | The worst output's loss stays inside and can be closed out, and sits outside the agreed red lines | A path outward exists, with a gate on it (human review, delayed sending, an amount cap) | One bad output reaches a customer, a regulator, or money, and cannot be taken back | Write the imagined text of the worst output word for word (e.g. "Your loss assessment payment is expected within three business days"). If you cannot write it, you have not thought about it seriously |
| 5 | ROI | What is the North Star outcome metric? What arithmetic turns the improvement into money? Who owns that bill? | An outcome metric + the conversion arithmetic + a person willing to carry that number in his own reporting | The metric is measurable, the conversion rough or the bill owner unconfirmed | Only activity metrics ("it launched", "92% accuracy") | The arithmetic itself, and the name of the person who owns the bill |

- Any question ≤2 (rule) → eliminated outright on that question. No weighting, no averaging, no "considering it in the round". Wherever you see a "weighted total" row, delete it. The only function of a weighted average is to translate "not feasible" into "ranked low", and a project ranked low still gets started in a year with budget to spare. "A weighted average is the excuse you reach for when you do not want to eliminate anything."
- A 3 (rule) → a conditional pass. Write the validation action that closes the gap into the sheet with a deadline. Missed by the deadline → it drops to 2.
- Blank evidence column → the score is a 2 (rule). Imagined scores are the table's most common forgery.
- Every candidate eliminated → a legitimate result. Write the conclusion as "back to discovery to find an opportunity", never "loosen the standard and screen again".
- Do not mix scales. 1–5 is you finding an opportunity's weakest link. Pass / concern / unsafe / useless is a real user on a single output.
- A row concludes in elimination → it needs three exits, or "not now" cannot be enforced at all.

| Exit | When it acts | What it settles |
|---|---|---|
| The gate | Before the report | Whether you have the standing to say what you are about to say |
| The referral route | After the report | Where the eliminated ask went and on whose books its ops cost lands. Three routes: refer to a standard tool (existing ticketing, BI, automation; write who configures it); refer to self-serve (a template and one training session, not a project); refer to an outside purchase (write who selects, who accepts, who owns ops after launch). Leave the ops-owner column blank and the name that fills in by default is yours |
| The recheck owner | Six months out | Whether this "not now" actually gets read again. Two more columns, who rechecks it and at which standing meeting. Default: hang the elimination table on the team's quarterly ask review (rule), read the eliminated rows each quarter, rerun the five questions where conditions have appeared, formally close the ones whose conditions never will and tell the proposer. The sheet is a team ledger, not a folder of yours, and it lives for years |

- Two candidates both pass the sheet → run the second sheet. The elimination table answers "is this worth doing", not "which one first", and in a shared pool "order" reads as "importance".

| Dimension | Question | How the order gets set |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence maturity | Whose lowest score is a conditional 3, and can the condition be met this quarter? | This-quarter conditions first, six-month conditions behind |
| Commitment hardness | Whose business owner has already claimed people and weekly hours by name? | Claimed first, verbal support behind |
| Organizational timing | Whose outcome metric can make the next business review? | The one that can make it first; the other is re-ranked next quarter |

- All three tie → back to Pain's bleed rate; whoever bleeds fastest goes first. Then put the opportunity cost on the same page: the candidates ranked behind, which row each is stuck on, which quarter each waits for, written for the sponsor and for the department that ranked behind. A trade-off that is not written out gets read as favoritism.
- The eliminated idea came from an executive → let the table speak, read only the evidence column, and hand him one sentence he can repeat upward. He does not need to be persuaded. He needs a reason he can pass on.

### Stage two, an extension

- A proposal to widen the slice arrives (one more user group, one more class of data, a platform, a favor from a peer department) → one row in the scope decision log, log it on the spot, sent back to the proposer within 48 hours (rule). The row is the proposer's answer; most proposals want an answer they can repeat, not a yes. Row: `<date> | <expansion> | <proposer> | <reason as a cost> | <revival event> | <status>`.
- The reason column holds an attitude ("not for now", "not a priority") → rewrite it as a cost ("eval doubles, pilot slips six weeks" (illustrative)).
- The revival column holds a date ("look again in Q3") → rewrite it as an event ("the first extension after the pilot North Star hits target"). Nothing happens when Q3 comes; when the event happens the proposer opens the page himself.
- The proposer sits in another department → cc the sponsor when you send the row back. The cc is not tattling. It sends a lateral request into the hands of the person with the authority to rank it.
- A "favor" will cost over half a day (rule) → it goes in the log too, proposer's name in, and the reason column states which item of this phase it crowds out. Most favors still get done; the row is so the time has somewhere to show.
- A milestone retrospective is on → reread the revival list. Entries whose condition is met either start or get the condition explicitly rewritten. Renege once and the log loses its credibility.

### Stage three, a phase close

- You are writing the impact memo's next-step options → every option other than the recommendation is a real option with "when to choose it" written. Two standing options: revival list items, stated by their revival condition (is it ripe, how far short), never by mood; and hold and watch, which always sits last, with the ceiling on the watching period and its cost stated. Hold and watch is the closeout form of "the evidence says not yet".
- Hold and watch comes due with no new evidence → "another month of watching" goes back through project approval. It does not extend itself.

### Stage four, a project at the door

- A candidate project arrives, before any demo → clear the three red lines first. Any one touched → do not take it, and however high the other dimensions score, nobody looks. Wait for the demo to say no and the budget is approved, the executive has reported it up, and sunk cost has taken everyone hostage, you included.

| # | Red line | Test question | The typical misjudgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Automated decisions at an irreversible-harm step | Can the harm from the single worst output be taken back? | Reading "a person signs at the end" as not automated. A signer with no time to actually look is nobody reviewing, and that is automated |
| 2 | A move of decision rights with no human oversight capacity behind it | After the move, is there still someone with the time to look, the ability to judge, and the authority to stop it? | Reading a timetable of "assist first, automate later" as oversight capacity already built |
| 3 | Outward-facing output in a regulatory grey zone | When this output causes a dispute, what do you answer the regulator with? | Reading "the regulator has not said no" as "the regulator said yes" |

- Red line discipline, one rule in three parts. Any one vetoes, and none enters the scoring; the meaning of a scoring sheet is trade-off, everything on it is compensable by a high score elsewhere, and a regulator does not fine you one unit less because strategic value scored 5. Adding or removing a red line is not the project team's vote; it is re-argued only when the external source changes (the regulator gets clear, the oversight capacity gets built, a reversal mechanism appears), and the re-argument leaves a trail. Keep them few, three or so (rule); every extra fake red line that could have been scored dilutes the veto force of the real ones. State red lines through regulation, compliance, audit and the AI governance policy wherever you can, so "this is not ours to vote on" is literally true.
- Red lines clear → score five dimensions 1–5, weakest link, no weighting, no averaging, evidence only (numbers, users' own words, samples). The five questions' Risk column is gone; it was promoted into a red line. Any dimension ≤2 (rule) does not enter the schedule and a re-evaluation condition gets written; a 3 (rule) is a conditional pass with condition and deadline. This notch is deliberately looser than the idea screen: assessing whether an organization can carry something runs one notch wider than assessing a single use case.

| Dimension | Test question | What a 1 looks like | What a 5 looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic value | Win it, and what does the organization get? One department's thanks, or agenda power and standard-setting power over a class of problem? | A single department's goodwill, no organization-level benefit you can state | It opens a repeatable class of problem, and the ROI arithmetic has a business owner behind it |
| Data readiness | How far do the key data exist, how reachable are they, how far can they be trusted? Have they been reconciled? | The key data do not exist, or no realistic path to them | Samples in hand, a reconciliation conclusion, a named data owner |
| Owner in place | Is there someone on the business side who signs for the outcome? Who backstops the errors, who maintains it after launch? | "Once it launches someone will look after it" | A named owner has been through the meeting and has claimed actions |
| Production path | Do review, oversight and change discipline get through? Or can only the demo live? | No path through security or compliance review, no oversight headcount in existence | The review path is agreed, the oversight role and its hours are committed |
| Reuse potential | What gets banked into the pattern library? How much cheaper is the second delivery? | Pure custom work, the judgment structure does not transfer | The skeleton transfers, and halving the cost of the second delivery has a basis |

- Owner in place has no name → score it 1 and it does not enter the schedule. No written agreement can force the business side to produce a signer, so force the name out with a trade: you want our people and our time, so give us the person who signs for the outcome after launch, name into the project approval resolution. What the claim buys him is that the system's priority and iteration schedule after launch are set by him.
- Verdict, by two predicates. All three red lines clear and no weak dimension → take it. Any red line touched → do not take it. Red lines clear with exactly one dimension at the floor → take it with conditions, and write three things on the spot: which dimension is short, who closes it by when, who re-scores it once closed. The three cannot be written → record it as not taken then and there; no ownerless candidate sits in the sheet.
- Take with conditions is the most used verdict inside. Projects you turn down do not disappear; the department has it built outside or buys a product, and when it errors the ops work routes back to you, because you are the only people who understand this stuff. Keeping the project on a path you can see is cheaper than picking it up afterward.

### The gate's force and its price

- The rubric is only talking to itself until you can name where its force comes from. Three routes, cheapest first: hang the three red lines and five dimensions on an existing review (project approval, architecture review, quarterly ask review; the gate then comes with an agenda, minutes and signatories, and is harder to route around than a new meeting); have the CIO or the responsible VP endorse the standard once at a meeting; write the three red lines into the company's AI governance policy, after which they are not your team's to vote on and not the proposer's to bid on.
- Stronger than a gate is a price. Showback or chargeback exists → the compute and labor bill is booked to the using department. It does not → charge the non-monetary price: every project supplies one full-time counterpart from the business side (rule), named, written into the project approval resolution. As long as the person with the idea has to put something up, inflation comes down by itself.

### The qualification arithmetic

- Turning down an executive's idea costs one person's goodwill, payable immediately, repairable with one report and one re-evaluation condition. Taking on an unqualified idea costs three months of the team's capacity plus AI's name at this company, payable in three months, unrepairable, and the next project is still yours to run. The arithmetic is more lopsided inside than outside and harder to enforce, because the goodwill bill you pay today and the other one the whole company pays next year.

## Procedures

**Screen an idea.**
1. Rewrite the idea as a workflow claim. Unwritable → out; tell the proposer which "who" is missing.
2. Score the five questions in order, Pain first. Evidence column takes numbers, users' own words, samples; hand back any adjective. Blank evidence = 2.
3. Apply the elimination rule. Any ≤2 → eliminated on that question, stop scoring, fill the rest afterward for the file. A 3 → write the closing action and deadline.
4. For each elimination write the referral route, the re-evaluation condition, the recheck owner and the standing meeting. Look up past rejections of the same ask (which department, which question it died on, where the money went) and put them in the evidence column; the proposer remembers that episode too.
5. More than one survivor → run the three-row ranking sheet; ties go to the fastest bleed rate.
6. Write the one page: conclusion with one sentence and one number; the elimination table; three lines per eliminated candidate (cause of death as "question X, N points", the single hardest piece of evidence, the re-evaluation condition); next step with time box, data access and staffing (the input to the charter negotiation); opportunity cost.
7. Deliver it in person, never by email. Eliminating someone's idea is a trust event, not an information transfer. Say "the evidence says not yet", never "I do not recommend it".
8. File the table where the team can find it in six months, and put the elimination rows on the quarterly ask review agenda.

**Rule on a project at intake.**
1. Fill in "where the gate's force comes from" first, naming the carrier meeting, the endorser, or the policy clause.
2. Run the three red lines with their test questions. Any one touched → do not take it; scoring stops.
3. Score the five dimensions with evidence. Owner in place with no name = 1.
4. Rule: take / do not take / take with conditions. For conditions write the short dimension, the closer and deadline, the re-scorer.
5. Score a vetoed candidate anyway for one use only: seeing which low dimensions the alternative path has to rebuild (reconcile the data, land a signer, wait for the regulator's line, build the oversight capacity). The steps are built from the low dimensions.
6. Enter every no in the kill register and send the row back to the proposer.

**Say the no in person.**
1. Affirm the goal. "You have not got the direction wrong, and this is the road we have been laying all along." Function: catch the goal, so the refusal earns the right to begin. "We get there sooner or later; what we settle today is which road and when." Function: switch the question from whether to how.
2. State the evidence. "This is the sheet we use for intake; on <dimension> the evidence is <numbers / users' own words / samples>." Function: let the sheet talk, you only read it. Put Risk in the other side's ledger: "The single worst output is <concrete scenario>. That account, <regulatory / brand / customer>, you know better than I do." Model probability is your ledger; regulation and brand are his.
3. Offer a path. "Not dropping it, rebuilding the steps. First do <the assist form one layer down>; <measurable threshold> met, <which class of decision> moves up one layer, each layer decided on its own." Function: turn the no into a yes with milestones. "I am putting this into the register; the revival condition is <an event, not a date>. When the day comes you will not have to raise it, I will." Function: turn the promise into a trail anyone can check.
4. Banned in step two: "the model will make mistakes", "AI is not mature enough" (both demote a judgment question into a technical one and invite "then get a stronger model"). Banned throughout: "the schedule is full" (delay is not refusal), "this cannot be done" (an unconditional no), and a "policy does not allow it" that cannot name which policy or clause (a shield, not a red line). A regulation number or policy clause you can name is fine.
5. Use your own words; keep the function of every sentence.

**Keep the kill register.**
1. Five columns per killed candidate: project, proposer, kill reason, revival condition (an event, not a date), retrospective conclusion a year later.
2. Name the sheet's owner (you, then whoever takes over intake). Reading the fifth column is a once-a-year pass (rule), two questions per row: did it get built later, here or elsewhere, and how did it go; did the revival condition come true, and once it did, did anyone re-score it.
3. Write one of three conclusions per row: the kill was right (built elsewhere, died of the cause predicted); the kill was wrong (the condition came true long ago with nobody re-scoring, or the predicted cause never happened); the evidence has changed (back through the rubric).
4. Whichever dimension the wrong kills cluster on → fix that dimension's test question. The judgment that judges intake also has to be judged.
5. The register stays empty for a long time → the gate is not working, not every judgment was right.

## Detectors

- If the Pain column holds nothing but "improve efficiency", the candidate was reasoned backward from technical capability to a scenario.
- If every departmental "AI requirements list" went straight into the backlog in the order received, you took the wishlist: wishes sorted by rank, not opportunities sorted by evidence.
- If the candidate ranked first is the one the executive's eyes lit up at, you measured narrative quality, not workflow leverage; on ROI settlement day the applause does not discount to a cent.
- If a running project cannot state the release criteria written down at intake, it got in on enthusiasm and a budget narrative.
- If, of the projects you said "let us look next quarter" about in the past six months, none was re-scored the next quarter, you used delay, not refusal, and rolled the interest on the no into the principal.
- If your red lines carry a score and can be averaged away by another dimension, they already are not red lines.
- If nobody can state last year's rejected candidate's revival condition, or whether it holds today, the fifth column is empty and refusal is the one judgment exempt from inspection.
- If the last refusal you sent has no sentence about what evidence would reopen it, attitude is all the other side received, and the next ask skips you and goes straight to a budget.
- If the referral route's ops-owner column is blank, the eliminated ask will error six months after someone else builds it and the ticket will land on you.
- If the elimination table lives in your local folder, it will not be there after you move on, and the same idea will be re-argued from memory.

## Key judgments

- "The cost of producing an AI idea has fallen close to zero, the cost of validating one is still expensive, and the mouth of the funnel must have discipline."
- "Any question scoring ≤2 is eliminated outright. A weighted average is the excuse you reach for when you do not want to eliminate anything."
- "Picking the use case is the deliverer's single highest-leverage decision, higher than any architecture decision."
- "'I am not doing it' is a position. 'The evidence says not yet' is a judgment."
- "Translate refusal from a relationship event into a judgment event."
- "Wait for the demo to say no and sunk cost has already taken everyone hostage."
- "A no must come with the conditions for a yes. An unconditional 'no' is a refusal. A conditional 'no' is a roadmap."
- "Once a red line is in the scoring sheet, it is no longer a red line."

## Vendor seat

The intake gate and its non-monetary price map to screening requests by quote: a vendor's quote alone screens out half the ideas, and the three red lines and the three steps to no hold as is. The vendor-side failure mode is still taking everything, because the gain from taking a job is booked at once to the taker and the cost of delivery later. A line in a contract is not a working mechanism.
